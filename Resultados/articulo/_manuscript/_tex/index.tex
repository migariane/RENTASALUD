\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background} Interactive tools linking income and life expectancy
at fine geographical scales are scarce for southern Europe.
\textbf{Methods} We linked INE census-section income data (48,262
section-years, 2015--2022) with BDLPA cohort life tables
(\textasciitilde637,000 individuals, follow-up 2011--2023) for
Andalusia, Ceuta, and Melilla. Correlation and clustering assessed the
income--longevity association. \textbf{Results} Life expectancy was 79.5
(men) and 84.4 years (women). Eliminating tumours would yield the
largest gain for men (3.93 years), while heart disease accounted for the
largest gain among women (2.89 years). The income--life expectancy
correlation across ten provinces was weak (r = 0.28, 95\%CI −0.42 to
0.77, P = 0.43; Spearman ρ = 0.24). Provincial EV ranged from 79.4
(Almería) to 82.7 years (Córdoba), a gradient of 3.3 years. An R Shiny
application integrates all findings in six interactive tabs.
\textbf{Conclusion} RENTASALUD provides an open tool for exploring
health inequalities. The weak provincial income--EV correlation is
compatible with the much steeper census-tract gradients reported
nationally. Aggregation at the province level attenuates the
association; income outliers (Ceuta, Melilla) further weaken it.
\textbf{Keywords:} health inequalities; life expectancy; spatial
epidemiology; interactive atlas; Shiny; Andalusia
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

It is well known that people with more money tend to live longer, and
this pattern holds across most European countries (Marmot 2010;
{Mackenbach et al.} 2015). In Southern Europe --- including Spain ---
the gap between rich and poor is somewhat narrower than in Northern or
Eastern Europe. This has been linked to the Mediterranean diet, strong
family and community networks, and universal healthcare ({Mackenbach et
al.} 2018; Regidor et al. 2012). But even in Spain, educational
disparities still account for roughly one in five deaths --- about
80,000 each year --- mostly from heart disease, lung cancer, respiratory
illnesses, and accidents ({Regidor et al.} 2014).

How big is the gap when we look at small areas like neighbourhoods or
census tracts? A recent national study by Redondo-Sánchez and colleagues
(Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022) found that women and men living in Spain's
most deprived neighbourhoods lived 3.2 and 3.8 years less, respectively,
than those in the wealthiest areas. This gap was wider for men and
varied by region, with northern Spain and provincial capitals faring
better.

Andalusia, home to about 8.5 million people, is Spain's largest region
and one of its most economically disadvantaged. Despite this, there are
few interactive tools that let people explore how income, demographics,
and life expectancy come together at the neighbourhood level. Most
existing maps are either too coarse (showing only whole provinces) or
lack the interactive features that would make them useful for
policymakers, researchers, or the public.

RENTASALUD was built to fill this gap. It is an open-source web atlas
that combines detailed census-section data from Spain's National
Statistics Institute (INE, 2015--2022) with life expectancy estimates
from the Longitudinal Database of the Andalusian Population (BDLPA),
which follows about 637,000 people from the 2011 census through to 2023.
The analysis covers the eight Andalusian provinces plus the autonomous
cities of Ceuta and Melilla. Our aims were: (i) to estimate life
expectancy by province and sex; (ii) to see how much longer people would
live if certain causes of death were eliminated; (iii) to examine the
relationship between income and life expectancy; and (iv) to build an
interactive tool that anyone can use.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Data sources}\label{data-sources}

We used two main datasets. The first comes from the INE's \emph{Atlas de
Distribución de Renta de los Hogares}, covering the eight Andalusian
provinces plus the autonomous cities of Ceuta and Melilla from 2015 to
2022. It includes over 48,000 census section--year observations with
information on median income, age structure, household composition, and
population size --- all at the neighbourhood level.

The second dataset is the BDLPA, which follows a random 10\% sample of
people who lived in Andalusia at the time of the 2011 census
(\textasciitilde637,000 individuals). By December 2023, the database
recorded whether each person was still alive, had moved away from
Andalusia, or had died, and if so, what caused their death (grouped into
10 categories: heart disease, cancer, diabetes, infections, lung
disease, digestive disease, nervous system disease, accidents,
alcohol-related causes, and other).

\subsection{How we calculated life
expectancy}\label{how-we-calculated-life-expectancy}

We used standard life table methods (Chiang 1968) to estimate how long a
newborn would be expected to live if current death rates stayed the
same. We grouped ages into standard 5-year bands (0--4, 5--9, \ldots,
85--89) with an open-ended band for 90 and over. We also calculated what
life expectancy would be if each cause of death were eliminated --- a
standard demographic technique called cause-deleted life tables
(Beltrán-Sánchez et al. 2008).

Life expectancy was calculated at the province level (8 provinces + 2
autonomous cities × 2 sexes = 20 tables). We could not calculate it at
the neighbourhood level because the BDLPA's individual IDs are
sequential numbers that contain no geographic information beyond the
province code.

\subsection{Analysing income and life
expectancy}\label{analysing-income-and-life-expectancy}

We looked at the relationship between average income and life expectancy
across the ten territories using two approaches: simple correlation
(Pearson and Spearman) and hierarchical clustering (to see which
provinces group together). These analyses are exploratory --- with only
ten data points, we have limited ability to detect patterns.

\subsection{Ethics}\label{ethics}

The study was approved by the University of Granada Ethics Committee
(SOCESVIDA, SICEIA-2025-000814, 7 March 2025).

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{How long do people in Andalusia
live?}\label{how-long-do-people-in-andalusia-live}

A baby born in Andalusia can expect to live about 79.5 years if male and
84.4 years if female --- a gap of nearly 5 years. This difference
reflects higher rates of heart disease, lung cancer, and accidents among
men. Life expectancy falls steadily with age: a 65-year-old man can
expect about 19.9 more years and a woman about 23.5; at age 85, the
remaining years are 7.3 for men and 8.4 for women.

Life expectancy varies across the ten territories (Table 1). Almería has
the lowest values (77.8 years for men, 81.1 for women), while Córdoba
leads (80.1 and 85.3). The gap is about 2.3 years for men and 4.2 years
for women. Ceuta and Melilla occupy intermediate positions: Ceuta (79.8
men, 84.7 women) and Melilla (79.8 men, 85.1 women) fall squarely within
the Andalusian range despite having substantially higher median incomes
(16,899 € and 15,443 € respectively vs.~the Andalusian average of
\textasciitilde13,300 €). This discrepancy --- higher income without
correspondingly higher life expectancy --- weakens the overall
income--EV association and reflects the distinctive economic structure
of these border cities.

\subsection{Does income relate to life
expectancy?}\label{does-income-relate-to-life-expectancy}

At the provincial level, the link between income and life expectancy was
weak and not statistically significant (r = 0.28, 95\% CI: −0.42 to
0.77, P = 0.43; Spearman ρ = 0.24, P = 0.51). The two autonomous cities
--- Ceuta (16,899 € median income, 82.2 years mean EV) and Melilla
(15,443 €, 82.4 years) --- act as leverage points: their incomes
substantially exceed the Andalusian average (12,699--14,338 €) yet their
life expectancy is mid-range, pulling the regression slope towards zero.
Removing them increases the correlation to r = 0.52 (P = 0.19). The
direction of association is positive but imprecise: with only ten data
points, the confidence interval spans from moderate negative to strong
positive. The 3.3-year spread in mean provincial EV (79.4 in Almería to
82.7 in Córdoba) confirms a real territorial gradient exists even at
this coarse scale.

This weak aggregate result does \emph{not} mean that income is unrelated
to health. When Redondo-Sánchez et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022)
looked at individual neighbourhoods rather than whole provinces, they
found a steep gradient (3.8 years for men, 3.2 for women between
deprivation extremes). The contrast illustrates how aggregation
attenuates association, especially when units differ in economic
structure. The practical implication is clear: neighbourhood-level data
are needed to detect the income--health gradient, and unit-level
solutions like RENTASALUD can help identify where interventions are most
needed.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity: with and without Ceuta and
Melilla}\label{sensitivity-with-and-without-ceuta-and-melilla}

Ceuta and Melilla are autonomous cities with economic structures
distinct from the eight Andalusian provinces --- their income is driven
by cross-border trade, a large public sector, and special tax regimes.
To assess their influence on the income--EV association, we repeated the
correlation analysis excluding them (Table 3).

\subsection{The RENTASALUD app}\label{the-rentasalud-app}

All the results above are available in an interactive R Shiny
application with six tabs: - \textbf{Explore} --- interactive maps of
income, demographics, and life expectancy by census section, with a year
slider to see changes over time. - \textbf{AI Assistant} --- ask
questions in plain Spanish, answered by a large language model running
locally or in the cloud. - \textbf{Time Series} --- see how any
indicator has evolved year by year for a selected province. -
\textbf{Relationships} --- scatter plots and bar charts exploring the
links between income and life expectancy. - \textbf{Mortality Causes}
--- interactive bar charts and tables showing how much each cause of
death shortens life expectancy. - \textbf{Methodology} --- definitions
of indicators, data sources, and citation information.

The app is open source. Code:
\href{https://github.com/migariane/RENTASALUD}{github.com/migariane/RENTASALUD}.
Online version: {[}shinyapps.io link{]}.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{What we found}\label{what-we-found}

RENTASALUD brings together, for the first time, detailed
neighbourhood-level income data with life expectancy estimates for
Andalusia, Ceuta, and Melilla. Life expectancy (79.5 for men, 84.4 for
women) is below the Spanish average, which is consistent with the
region's lower income levels. Cancer and heart disease are the main
drivers of early death --- together accounting for about 7.1 years of
potential life expectancy gain in men and 5.7 in women. These findings
highlight the continued importance of screening programmes,
cardiovascular prevention, and smoking cessation. The larger impact of
cancer in men (+1.1 years) reflects Spain's history of higher smoking
rates among men, although this gap is closing in younger generations.

\subsection{Why the income--health link attenuates at the provincial
level}\label{why-the-incomehealth-link-attenuates-at-the-provincial-level}

The correlation between income and life expectancy at the provincial
level (r = 0.28, P = 0.43) is weak and imprecise, contrasting with the
steep and precise gradient found at the neighbourhood level (a gap of
3.8 years for men and 3.2 for women between the most and least deprived
areas) (Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022). Two factors contribute to this
attenuation. First, aggregating data from thousands of census tracts
into ten provincial units inevitably reduces variance and statistical
power. Second, Ceuta and Melilla --- autonomous cities with high median
incomes (16,899 € and 15,443 €) driven by cross-border trade and
public-sector employment --- have mid-range life expectancy (82.2 and
82.4 years), pulling the provincial correlation toward zero. When these
two cities are excluded, the correlation rises to r = 0.52 among the
eight Andalusian provinces, suggesting that the income--EV association
may operate differently in territories with distinct economic
structures. The 3.3-year spread in provincial life expectancy (79.4 to
82.7 years) mirrors, at the aggregate scale, the direction of the
neighbourhood-level gradient. The Southern European pattern of smaller
health inequalities ({Mackenbach et al.} 2018; Regidor et al. 2012) does
not mean there is no inequality --- it means the gradient operates at a
finer scale and may be softened by factors like the Mediterranean diet,
social networks, and universal healthcare. The practical implication is
clear: policies should target the most disadvantaged neighbourhoods, not
whole provinces. RENTASALUD is designed to help identify exactly where
those neighbourhoods are.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}\label{strengths-and-limitations}

The main strength of this study is that it combines individual-level
data on nearly 637,000 people followed for 12 years with fine-grained
socioeconomic information, all within an open-source, interactive
platform that includes an AI-powered query interface.

However, there are important limitations. First, life expectancy could
only be calculated at the province level --- the single biggest
limitation. Future work should link the BDLPA to the municipal register
(Padrón Continuo) to recover neighbourhood codes and allow small-area
Bayesian estimation. Second, life tables assume that current death rates
remain constant, which may not be true during periods of rapid change.
Third, sample sizes are small at extreme ages (children under 5 and
people over 90) when broken down by province and sex. Fourth, the income
data cover 2015--2022, while mortality follow-up covers 2011--2023,
creating a partial mismatch. Fifth, the cause-deletion method assumes
that causes of death are independent, which they are not --- eliminating
one cause does not simply add up with eliminating another.

topic}\label{what-is-already-known-on-this-topic}

\begin{itemize}

\item
  Southern Europe has somewhat smaller health inequalities than Northern

\item
  Few interactive tools exist that let people explore income and life

\item

\end{itemize}

\section{What this study adds}\label{what-this-study-adds}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  RENTASALUD is the first open-source interactive atlas of
  neighbourhood-level income and life expectancy for Andalusia, Ceuta,
  and Melilla.
\item
  At the province level, the income--life expectancy correlation is weak
  (r = 0.28, P = 0.43), with a 3.3-year spread in mean EV (79.4 in
  Almería to 82.7 in Córdoba). Ceuta and Melilla are income outliers
  that weaken the aggregate association.
\item
  Cancer and heart disease together account for about 7.1 years of
  potential life expectancy gain in men and 5.7 in women.
\item
  An AI-powered natural language interface lets anyone explore the data
  without technical training.
\end{itemize}
