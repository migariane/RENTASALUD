\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background} Interactive tools linking income and life expectancy
at fine geographical scales are scarce for southern Europe.
\textbf{Methods} We linked INE census-section income data (48,262
section-years, 2015--2022) with BDLPA cohort life tables
(\textasciitilde637,000 individuals, follow-up 2011--2023) for
Andalusia, Ceuta, and Melilla. Correlation and clustering assessed the
income--longevity association. \textbf{Results} Life expectancy was 79.5
(men) and 84.4 years (women). Eliminating tumours would yield the
largest gain for men (3.93 years), while heart disease accounted for the
largest gain among women (2.89 years). The income--life expectancy
correlation across ten provinces was weak (r = 0.28, 95\%CI −0.42 to
0.77, P = 0.43; Spearman ρ = 0.24). Provincial EV ranged from 79.4
(Almería) to 82.7 years (Córdoba), a gradient of 3.3 years. An R Shiny
application integrates all findings in six interactive tabs.
\textbf{Conclusion} RENTASALUD provides an open tool for exploring
health inequalities. The weak provincial income--EV correlation is
compatible with the much steeper census-tract gradients reported
nationally. Aggregation at the province level attenuates the
association; income outliers (Ceuta, Melilla) further weaken it.
\textbf{Keywords:} health inequalities; life expectancy; spatial
epidemiology; interactive atlas; Shiny; Andalusia \textbf{* Equal
contribution.} GRG and PMG contributed equally as co-first authors.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

People with higher incomes live longer, on average, across most European
countries (Marmot 2010; {Mackenbach et al.} 2015). In Southern Europe
the gap between rich and poor is narrower than in the north or east, a
pattern that researchers have connected to the Mediterranean diet,
strong family and community ties, and universal healthcare ({Mackenbach
et al.} 2018; Regidor et al. 2012). Even so, educational disparities in
Spain still drive roughly one in five deaths each year --- some 80,000
people --- mostly from heart disease, lung cancer, respiratory illness,
and accidents ({Regidor et al.} 2014).

When the analysis moves down to small areas like neighbourhoods or
census tracts, the picture sharpens. Redondo-Sánchez and colleagues
(Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022) found that Spanish women and men in the
most deprived neighbourhoods lived 3.2 and 3.8 fewer years,
respectively, than those in the wealthiest areas. The gap was wider for
men and varied across the country; the north and provincial capitals did
better.

Andalusia is Spain's largest region, home to eight and a half million
people, and one of its poorest. Plenty of maps exist, but nearly all of
them are either too coarse (whole provinces) or too static to be useful
to a town councillor, a public health officer, or a curious resident.
The aim of our project, RENTASALUD, was to fix that: to build a single
tool where income, demographics, and life expectancy can be explored
together at the neighbourhood scale, with a year slider and an AI
assistant that answers questions in plain language.

RENTASALUD is an open source web atlas. It draws on census-section data
from Spain's National Statistics Institute (INE, 2015--2022) and life
expectancy estimates from the Longitudinal Database of the Andalusian
Population (BDLPA), which tracks roughly 637,000 people from the 2011
census through to the end of 2023. The analysis covers the eight
Andalusian provinces plus Ceuta and Melilla. We set out to estimate life
expectancy by province and sex, to quantify how much each cause of death
shortens life, to examine whether provincial income predicts longevity,
and to put it all in an interactive tool that requires no statistical
training to use.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Data sources}\label{data-sources}

The income and demographic data come from the INE \emph{Atlas de
Distribución de Renta de los Hogares} (Instituto Nacional de Estadística
2024), which covers the eight Andalusian provinces and the autonomous
cities of Ceuta and Melilla for the period 2015--2022. It contains over
48,000 census section--year observations: median income, age structure,
household size and composition, and total population.

The mortality data come from the BDLPA (Instituto de Estadística y
Cartografía de Andalucía 2024), a random 10\% sample of everyone who
lived in Andalusia at the 2011 census, followed through December 2023.
For each person the database records whether they were still alive, had
moved away, or had died. When a death occurred, the cause is coded into
one of ten broad groups: heart and circulatory disease, malignant
tumours, diabetes and endocrine conditions, infectious disease,
respiratory disease, digestive disease, nervous system disorders,
external causes (accidents and suicide), alcohol-related causes, and all
other causes.

\subsection{Life expectancy
estimation}\label{life-expectancy-estimation}

We used standard abridged life table methods (Chiang 1968) with 5-year
age bands (0--4, 5--9, \ldots, 85--89) and an open final band for ages
90 and over. Cause-deleted life tables (Beltrán-Sánchez et al. 2008)
were computed to estimate the gain in life expectancy at birth that
would result from removing each cause, one at a time, using Chiang's
proportional-risk adjustment. Life expectancy was calculated at the
province level (8 provinces + 2 autonomous cities × 2 sexes = 20 life
tables). Census-tract estimation was not possible because the BDLPA
identification numbers are sequential codes with no geographic
information beyond the two-digit province code.

\subsection{Income and life
expectancy}\label{income-and-life-expectancy}

We examined the province-level association between median income and
mean life expectancy using Pearson and Spearman correlations, plus
hierarchical clustering to explore groupings. With only ten data points
the analysis is exploratory and has limited statistical power. As a
sensitivity check we repeated the correlation after removing Ceuta and
Melilla --- autonomous cities whose economies depend heavily on
cross-border trade and public-sector employment, making them
structurally different from the Andalusian provinces.

\subsection{Ethics}\label{ethics}

The study was approved by the University of Granada Ethics Committee
(SOCESVIDA, SICEIA-2025-000814, 7 March 2025).

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Life expectancy in
Andalusia}\label{life-expectancy-in-andalusia}

A newborn in Andalusia can expect to live about 79.5 years if male and
84.4 if female, a gap of just under 5 years. The difference reflects
higher death rates from heart disease, lung cancer, and accidents among
men. As age increases, remaining life expectancy drops in the expected
pattern: at 65, a man has about 19.9 years left and a woman about 23.5,
while at 85 the remaining years are 7.3 and 8.4.

Life expectancy differs across the ten territories (Table 1). Almería
sits at the bottom for both sexes (77.8 years for men, 81.1 for women);
Córdoba is at the top (80.1 and 85.3). The gap between lowest and
highest is roughly 2.3 years for men and 4.2 for women. Ceuta and
Melilla fall in the middle of the range: Ceuta records 79.8 years for
men and 84.7 for women, Melilla 79.8 and 85.1. Their incomes (€16,899
and €15,443) are well above the Andalusian average of roughly €13,300,
yet their life expectancy is not proportionally higher, a mismatch that
pulls the overall income--EV correlation downwards.

\subsection{Income and life
expectancy}\label{income-and-life-expectancy-1}

At the province level, the linear association between median income and
mean life expectancy was weak and not statistically distinguishable from
zero (r = 0.28, 95\% CI: −0.42 to 0.77, P = 0.43; Spearman ρ = 0.24, P =
0.51). Ceuta (€16,899 median income, 82.2 years mean EV) and Melilla
(€15,443, 82.4 years) stand out: their incomes substantially exceed the
Andalusian range of €12,699--€14,338, yet their life expectancy sits
squarely in the middle, pulling the regression line toward flat.
Removing these two cities lifts the correlation to r = 0.52 (P = 0.19).
The sign is positive, but with ten data points the confidence interval
is wide enough to be compatible with anything from a moderate negative
association to a strong positive one. The 3.3-year spread in mean
provincial life expectancy (79.4 in Almería to 82.7 in Córdoba) does
indicate a real territorial gradient, even if its statistical expression
is noisy.

The weak aggregate association does not imply that income is irrelevant
to health. At the neighbourhood level, Redondo-Sánchez et al.
(Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022) found a steep, precise gradient: 3.8 years
for men and 3.2 for women between the extremes of deprivation. The gap
between a provincial r of 0.28 and a neighbourhood-level gulf of 3--4
years is exactly what one would expect when summarising thousands of
census tracts into ten administrative units, particularly when two of
those units operate under different economic rules. Neighbourhood-scale
data are needed to see the gradient; tools like RENTASALUD are designed
to show where the sharpest inequalities lie.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to Ceuta and
Melilla}\label{sensitivity-to-ceuta-and-melilla}

cross-border trade, a large public sector, and special tax arrangements,
rather than by the agricultural and service activities that dominate
Andalusia. To gauge their influence, we repeated the correlation

\subsection{The RENTASALUD
application}\label{the-rentasalud-application}

All the results described above are accessible through an interactive R
Shiny application with six tabs:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Explore} --- choropleth maps of income, demographics, and life
  expectancy at the census-section level, with a year slider
  (2015--2022).
\item
  \textbf{AI Assistant} --- natural-language queries in Spanish,
  answered by a large language model running locally on the user's
  machine or via a cloud API.
\item
  \textbf{Time Series} --- yearly trends for any indicator, filtered by
  province.
\item
  \textbf{Relationships} --- scatter plots and bar charts exploring
  income and life expectancy, with and without Ceuta and Melilla.
\item
  \textbf{Mortality Causes} --- interactive charts and tables showing
  gains from eliminating each cause.
\item
  \textbf{Methodology} --- indicator definitions, data sources, and
  citation details.
\end{itemize}

The full analysis code is available at
\href{https://github.com/migariane/RENTASALUD}{github.com/migariane/RENTASALUD}.
The live application runs at
\href{https://watzile.shinyapps.io/RENTA/}{watzile.shinyapps.io/RENTA}.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{What we found}\label{what-we-found}

Life expectancy in Andalusia, Ceuta, and Melilla sits below the Spanish
average, which fits with the region's lower income levels. Cancer and
heart disease are the main drivers of premature death. The larger cancer
burden in men (an extra 1.1 years of potential gain) mirrors Spain's
historical smoking patterns, a gap that is closing in younger
generations.

\subsection{Why the provincial income--EV link is
weak}\label{why-the-provincial-incomeev-link-is-weak}

The province-level correlation (r = 0.28, P = 0.43) is weak and
imprecise. Two things flatten it. The first is scale: thousands of
census tracts aggregated into ten units, which is bound to shrink
variance and lose power. The second is Ceuta and Melilla: cities with
high median incomes that do not translate into proportionally longer
lives. When they are removed, the correlation among the eight Andalusian
provinces rises to r = 0.52, enough to be suggestive but still not
conclusive.

A 3.3-year spread in provincial life expectancy (79.4 to 82.7) is real
and, at this coarse resolution, runs in the expected direction. The
steeper gradients that Redondo-Sánchez et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al.
2022) found at the neighbourhood level suggest that within-province
differences probably exceed what we can detect here. The Southern
European pattern of compressed health inequalities ({Mackenbach et al.}
2018; Regidor et al. 2012) does not mean inequality is absent: it means
the signal operates at a finer scale and may be dampened by diet, social
networks, and healthcare access. Policy efforts that target the most
disadvantaged neighbourhoods, rather than entire provinces, are more
likely to make a measurable difference.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}\label{strengths-and-limitations}

The study links individual-level follow-up on nearly 637,000 people over
12 years to neighbourhood socioeconomic data. Everything is open source
and runs through an interactive platform with a built-in
natural-language interface.

The most important limitation is geographic: life expectancy could only
be estimated at province level because the BDLPA person identifiers
carry no sub-provincial geographic code. Linking the BDLPA to the
municipal register (Padrón Continuo) would unlock small-area estimation
and is, in our view, the most worthwhile next step. Other limitations
include the assumption of constant mortality rates, small cell sizes at
extreme ages when stratified by province and sex, a partial mismatch
between the income window (2015--2022) and the mortality follow-up
(2011--2023), and the fact that cause-deleted life tables treat causes
as independent when in practice they are not.

\section{What is already known on this
topic}\label{what-is-already-known-on-this-topic}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In Spain, residents of the most deprived neighbourhoods live 3 to 4
  years less than those in the wealthiest.
\item
  Southern Europe has smaller health inequalities than Northern or
  Eastern Europe.
\item
  Fine-scale interactive tools that let users explore income alongside
  life expectancy are rare.
\item
  Autonomous cities with border-trade economies (Ceuta, Melilla) may sit
  outside typical income--health gradients.
\end{itemize}

\section{What this study adds}\label{what-this-study-adds}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  RENTASALUD is the first open source interactive atlas of
  neighbourhood-level income and life expectancy for Andalusia, Ceuta,
  and Melilla.
\item
  At province level, the income--life expectancy correlation is weak (r
  = 0.28, P = 0.43) and doubles when Ceuta and Melilla are excluded (r =
  0.52).
\item
  Mean provincial life expectancy spans 3.3 years (79.4 in Almería to
  82.7 in Córdoba), and the gap is wider for women.
\item
  Cancer and cardiovascular disease together account for 7.1 years of
  potential life expectancy gain in men and 5.7 in women.
\item
  A built-in AI assistant lets users query the data in everyday language
  with no technical training.
\end{itemize}
