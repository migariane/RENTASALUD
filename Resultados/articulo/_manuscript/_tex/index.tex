\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background:} Social inequalities in health are a persistent
challenge in public health. In Andalusia, southern Spain, the
availability of granular, interactive tools to explore the spatial
distribution of income, demographic indicators, and life expectancy at
the census section level remains limited. This article presents
RENTASALUD, a web-based interactive atlas that integrates longitudinal
socioeconomic data from the Spanish National Institute of Statistics
(INE) with life expectancy estimates derived from the Longitudinal
Database of the Andalusian Population (BDLPA).

\textbf{Methods:} We constructed a dataset of 49,888 census section-year
observations covering all eight Andalusian provinces from 2015 to 2022,
including median income, age structure, household composition, and
population counts. Life expectancy at birth was estimated using period
life tables following Chiang's (1968) method, applied to the 2011 census
cohort (\textasciitilde637,000 individuals) with mortality follow-up
through 2023 from the BDLPA. Life expectancy was computed at the
provincial level because the BDLPA individual identifier is a sequential
record number that does not encode census section codes. We further
decomposed gains in life expectancy by 10 major cause-of-death groups.
The interactive web application was built in R Shiny.

\textbf{Results:} Life expectancy at birth in Andalusia is estimated at
79.5 years for men (range across provinces: 78.8--79.6) and 84.4 years
for women (range: 83.7--84.6). The leading causes of mortality burden
are tumours (gain of 3.93 years for men, 2.83 for women if eliminated)
and circulatory diseases (3.15 and 2.89 years, respectively). The
Pearson correlation between median income and life expectancy at the
provincial level was weak and non-significant (r = 0.21, 95\% CI: -0.59
to 0.81, p = 0.62; Spearman's \(\rho\) = 0.07, p = 0.87). Hierarchical
cluster analysis identified two province clusters (higher-income:
Málaga, Sevilla, Córdoba, Granada; lower-income: Almería, Cádiz, Huelva,
Jaén), although the difference was not statistically significant.
Generalised additive models showed no evidence of non-linearity (EDF =
1.32, p = 0.48). The Shiny application provides interactive maps,
time-series sliders, and a natural-language AI assistant for querying
the data.

\textbf{Discussion:} RENTASALUD offers public health researchers,
policymakers, and the general public an accessible tool for exploring
territorial inequalities in health determinants and outcomes in
Andalusia. The positive association between income and life expectancy
underscores the need for targeted interventions in deprived areas. The
main limitation is the provincial-level geographical resolution of life
expectancy estimates. Future work will incorporate small-area estimation
models to produce census-section-level life expectancy.

\textbf{Keywords:} health inequalities, life expectancy, spatial
epidemiology, interactive atlas, R Shiny, Andalusia
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Social inequalities in health represent one of the most persistent
challenges in public health research and policy. A large body of
evidence has documented a strong and consistent association between
socioeconomic deprivation and mortality, morbidity, and life expectancy
across various geographical contexts (Marmot 2010; {Mackenbach et al.}
2015). Within Europe, a distinctive pattern has been observed: Southern
European countries---including Spain---consistently exhibit
\textbf{smaller educational and socioeconomic inequalities in mortality}
compared to Northern, Western, and Eastern European regions ({Mackenbach
et al.} 2018; Regidor et al. 2012). This ``Southern European pattern''
has been attributed to cultural factors such as widespread adherence to
the Mediterranean diet, the ubiquitous presence of strong social
networks, and a more equitable distribution of certain health-related
behaviours (Regidor et al. 2012).

Despite these smaller relative inequalities, the absolute burden remains
substantial. Recent estimates suggest that close to one in five deaths
in Spain (approximately 80,000 per year) can be attributed to
educational inequalities, with the largest impacts concentrated at young
and middle ages, and driven primarily by cardiovascular diseases,
smoking-related cancers, respiratory diseases, and external causes
({Regidor et al.} 2014).

At the census tract level---the finest administrative unit for which the
Spanish National Institute of Statistics (INE) publishes disaggregated
socioeconomic data---the evidence on life expectancy gradients is
particularly compelling. A landmark study by Redondo-Sánchez,
Luque-Fernández et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022), published in
\emph{Scientific Reports} (PMID: 36114247), constructed sex- and
age-specific life tables by socioeconomic deprivation at the census
tract level across all of Spain for the period 2011--2013. Using the
Spanish Deprivation Index (SDI) grouped into quintiles, the authors
reported that:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Women and men in the \textbf{most deprived} census tracts lived
  \textbf{3.2 and 3.8 years less}, respectively, than those in the least
  deprived areas.
\item
  The socioeconomic gradient in life expectancy was \textbf{more
  pronounced among men} than women.
\item
  \textbf{Geographic variation} was substantial: higher life expectancy
  was observed in northern regions of Spain and in provincial capitals
  compared to rural areas, while southern regions including Andalusia
  showed lower values across all deprivation quintiles.
\item
  The life tables produced were specifically designed to enable
  estimation of \textbf{cancer relative survival and other health
  outcome measures} stratified by socioeconomic status, providing a
  crucial methodological foundation for subsequent research on health
  inequalities (Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022).
\end{itemize}

Andalusia, the most populous autonomous community in Spain
(approximately 8.5 million inhabitants), is characterised by
historically lower income levels and higher unemployment rates than the
national average. Understanding how these socioeconomic conditions
translate into health outcomes at fine geographical scales is essential
for designing targeted public health interventions. However, most
available tools either provide data at coarse administrative levels
(province or region) or lack interactive visualisation capabilities that
would make them accessible to non-specialist audiences. Furthermore,
while studies like Redondo-Sánchez et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022)
have produced life tables by deprivation at the national level, there is
a need for region-specific interactive tools that allow policymakers,
researchers, and the public to explore both socioeconomic determinants
and mortality outcomes in a unified interface.

The RENTASALUD project was conceived to address this gap by developing
an interactive web-based atlas that integrates two complementary data
sources: (1) granular socioeconomic and demographic indicators at the
census section level from the INE's \emph{Atlas de Distribución de Renta
de los Hogares} for the period 2015--2022, and (2) life expectancy
estimates derived from the Longitudinal Database of the Andalusian
Population (BDLPA), a large-scale census-linked cohort study covering
the 2011 census with mortality follow-up through 2023.

In this article, we describe the construction of the RENTASALUD
application, present the main findings on socioeconomic inequalities and
life expectancy in Andalusia, discuss the association between income and
life expectancy in the context of the existing literature, and outline
the methodological challenges and limitations of the current
implementation.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Study Area and Data
Sources}\label{study-area-and-data-sources}

The study area comprises all census sections in the eight provinces of
Andalusia, southern Spain: Almería, Cádiz, Córdoba, Granada, Huelva,
Jaén, Málaga, and Sevilla. Census sections are the smallest
administrative units for which the INE publishes disaggregated
demographic and socioeconomic data, with a population typically ranging
from 1,000 to 2,500 inhabitants.

We used two main data sources:

\subsubsection{Socioeconomic and Demographic Data
(INE)}\label{socioeconomic-and-demographic-data-ine}

Individual-level data on median income, age structure, household
composition, and population counts by census section were obtained from
the INE's \emph{Atlas de Distribución de Renta de los Hogares}
(Household Income Distribution Atlas) for the period 2015--2022. The
data were provided in a longitudinal format
(\texttt{datos\_rentapop\_long.RData}) containing 49,888 census
section-year observations across 14 variables:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Median income} (Renta\_Mediana\_UC): median disposable income
  per consumption unit, calculated according to the modified OECD
  equivalence scale (1.0 for the first adult, 0.5 for additional adults
  aged ≥14 years, 0.3 for children under 14).
\item
  \textbf{Demographic indicators}: mean age, percentage of population
  under 18 and over 65 years, proportion of Spanish and foreign-born
  residents.
\item
  \textbf{Household characteristics}: average household size, percentage
  of single-person households.
\item
  \textbf{Population counts}: total population by census section and
  year.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Mortality and Life Expectancy Data
(BDLPA)}\label{mortality-and-life-expectancy-data-bdlpa}

The Longitudinal Database of the Andalusian Population (BDLPA) is a
census-linked cohort that follows all individuals enumerated in the 2011
Census of Andalusia. For this study, we used a 10\% representative
sample of the census (\textasciitilde637,000 individuals) with mortality
follow-up through 31 December 2023. The database provides:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Individual-level records with age at census, sex, and province of
  residence.
\item
  Survival status at follow-up end (alive, emigrated from Andalusia, or
  deceased).
\item
  Cause of death coded according to 10 major groups: circulatory
  diseases, malignant tumours, endocrine diseases, infectious diseases,
  respiratory diseases, digestive diseases, nervous system diseases,
  external causes, alcohol-related causes, and remaining causes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Life Expectancy
Estimation}\label{life-expectancy-estimation}

Life expectancy at birth was estimated using standard period life table
methods (Chiang 1968). For each sex and geographical unit, we
constructed abridged life tables with 5-year age intervals from 0--4 to
85--89, with an open-ended interval at age 90+ (120 years, the standard
demographic convention).

The mortality rate for age interval \([x, x+n)\) was calculated as:

\[{}_n M_x = \frac{D_x}{P_x}\]

where \(D_x\) is the number of deaths and \(P_x\) the person-years of
exposure in that interval.

The probability of dying within the interval, \({}_n q_x\), was derived
using the standard transformation:

\[{}_n q_x = \frac{n \cdot {}_n M_x}{1 + (n - {}_n a_x) \cdot {}_n M_x}\]

where \({}_n a_x\) represents the average number of person-years lived
by those dying in the interval, assumed to be \(n/2\) for all age groups
except the open-ended interval, where \({}_\infty q_{90} = 1\).

Life expectancy at age \(x\) was then calculated as:

\[e_x = \frac{T_x}{l_x}\]

where \(l_x\) is the number of survivors at exact age \(x\) (starting
from a radix of 100,000) and \(T_x\) is the total number of person-years
lived above age \(x\).

\subsubsection{Cause-of-Death
Decomposition}\label{cause-of-death-decomposition}

To estimate the gain in life expectancy that would result from
eliminating a specific cause of death, we applied Chiang's method
(Chiang 1968). For each age interval, we calculated the proportion of
deaths not attributable to cause \(c\):

\[{}_n R^{(c)}_x = 1 - \frac{{}_n D^{(c)}_x}{{}_n D_x}\]

The adjusted probability of surviving the interval was then computed as:

\[{}_n p^{(c)}_x = ({}_n p_x)^{{}_n R^{(c)}_x}\]

where \({}_n p_x = 1 - {}_n q_x\) is the observed probability of
surviving the interval. This adjustment reflects the counterfactual
scenario where cause \(c\) is eliminated and the remaining causes
maintain their relative incidence.

\subsubsection{Geographical Resolution}\label{geographical-resolution}

Life expectancy was estimated at the provincial level (eight provinces)
and by sex (male/female) for a total of 16 life tables. It was not
possible to compute life expectancy at the census section level for two
reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  The BDLPA individual identifier is a six-digit sequential record
  number that contains no geographical information beyond the province
  code.
\item
  The housing file (\texttt{smv11cau.txt}) does contain detailed
  geographical codes, but these are in a proprietary format incompatible
  with the standard INE 10-digit census section code (CUSEC), and the
  file only covers approximately 22\% of the sample individuals.
\end{enumerate}

Consequently, all census sections within a province share the same life
expectancy value in the application.

\subsubsection{Income--Life Expectancy Association: Clustering and
Non-Linear
Analysis}\label{incomelife-expectancy-association-clustering-and-non-linear-analysis}

To explore the relationship between median income and life expectancy at
the provincial level, we conducted three complementary analyses. First,
we computed the \textbf{Pearson correlation coefficient} between
provincial mean income (averaged over all census sections and years) and
the estimated life expectancy at birth for each province, with
corresponding 95\% confidence intervals and p-values. Given the limited
number of observations (n = 8 provinces), we also computed
\textbf{Spearman's rank correlation} as a robust alternative less
sensitive to outliers.

Second, we performed a \textbf{hierarchical cluster analysis} to
identify groups of provinces with similar income--life expectancy
profiles. Using Euclidean distances on the standardised (z-score)
bivariate space of mean income and life expectancy, we applied Ward's
minimum variance linkage criterion. The optimal number of clusters was
determined using the elbow method on the within-cluster sum of squares
and confirmed by the silhouette coefficient.

Third, we explored potential \textbf{non-linear relationships} by
fitting a generalised additive model (GAM) with a penalised cubic
regression spline for the income term:

\[\text{EV}_i = \alpha + s(\text{Income}_i) + \varepsilon_i\]

where \(s(\cdot)\) is a smooth function with basis dimension \(k = 4\)
(limited by the small sample size), estimated by penalised likelihood
with generalised cross-validation (GCV) for smoothness parameter
selection. The effective degrees of freedom (EDF) of the smooth term
were used to assess departure from linearity: EDF \(\approx 1\)
indicates a linear relationship, while EDF \(> 1.5\) suggests
non-linearity. We compared the GAM fit to the linear model using
Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) and an F-test for the smooth term.

We acknowledge that the statistical power to detect any
association---linear or non-linear---is severely limited with only eight
observations. These analyses are presented as exploratory and
descriptive, and the results should be interpreted with caution. The
primary value of the RENTASALUD application lies in the integrated
visualisation of these relationships through interactive scatter plots,
cluster dendrograms, and loess smoothing curves available in the
\emph{Relaciones} tab.

\subsection{Web Application
Development}\label{web-application-development}

The RENTASALUD interactive atlas was developed using R Shiny (R Core
Team 2024), with the following key components:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{global.R}}: Data loading, preparation, and shared
  functions (format\_value, get\_palette, build\_tooltip,
  generate\_narrative).
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{app.R}}: User interface and server logic with five
  navigation tabs:

  \begin{enumerate}
  \def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Explorar} (Explore): interactive choropleth map with year
    slider and indicator selector, value boxes, and narrative context.
  \item
    \emph{Asistente IA} (AI Assistant): natural-language query interface
    powered by local (Ollama) or cloud (OpenAI, Gemini) large language
    models via the \texttt{querychat} and \texttt{ellmer} R packages.
  \item
    \emph{Relaciones} (Relationships): scatter plot of income vs.~life
    expectancy with linear regression line, correlation coefficient
    display, and grouped bar chart of life expectancy by province and
    sex.
  \item
    \emph{Causas Mortalidad} (Mortality Causes): analysis of life
    expectancy gains by cause of death, with horizontal bar charts,
    interactive data tables, and age-band life expectancy curves.
  \item
    \emph{Metodología} (Methodology): definitions of all indicators and
    data source documentation.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

The application is deployed at \textbf{{[}shinyapps.io link to be
inserted{]}} and the source code is available at \textbf{{[}GitHub
repository link to be inserted{]}}.

\subsection{Income--Life Expectancy
Association}\label{incomelife-expectancy-association}

Figure 1 presents the relationship between median income and life
expectancy at the provincial level. The Pearson correlation coefficient
is \textbf{r = 0.21} (R\^{}2 = 0.04, 95\% CI: -0.59 to 0.81, p = 0.62),
indicating a weak positive association that is not statistically
significant. The Spearman rank correlation was similarly weak and
non-significant (\(\rho\) = 0.07, p = 0.87). Provinces with higher
median income do not consistently show higher life expectancy at this
aggregated level, although the small number of observations (n = 8
provinces) severely limits the statistical power to detect any
association.

The \textbf{hierarchical cluster analysis} revealed two distinct
clusters of provinces. The first cluster (higher income, moderately
higher life expectancy) included Málaga, Sevilla, Córdoba, and Granada,
with mean income ranging from €13,341 to €14,338 and life expectancy
from 81.75 to 82.10 years. The second cluster (lower income, slightly
lower life expectancy) included Almería, Cádiz, Huelva, and Jaén, with
mean income ranging from €12,699 to €13,172 and life expectancy from
81.25 to 81.85 years. This clustering pattern, while visually
discernible, did not reach statistical significance given the small
sample size.

The \textbf{GAM analysis} yielded an effective degrees of freedom (EDF)
of 1.32 for the income smooth term, suggesting only a mild departure
from linearity. The GAM fit did not significantly improve over the
linear model (\(\Delta\)AIC = -0.8, F-test p = 0.48). The loess smoother
shown in Figure 1 visualises the non-parametric trend, which suggests a
potential plateau or threshold effect at intermediate income levels, but
this pattern is driven by individual provinces and should not be
over-interpreted.

Collectively, these analyses underscore a key methodological finding: at
the provincial level of aggregation, the income--longevity gradient is
weak, non-significant, and shows only modest evidence of non-linearity.
This stands in marked contrast to the strong and statistically
significant gradients observed at finer geographical scales
(Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022), highlighting the critical role of spatial
scale in health inequality research.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The RENTASALUD project provides an integrated, interactive tool for
exploring the spatial distribution of income, demography, and life
expectancy in Andalusia. The main substantive findings are consistent
with the existing literature on health inequalities in Spain and Europe.

\subsection{Principal Findings}\label{principal-findings}

Life expectancy in Andalusia (79.5 years for men, 84.4 for women) is
slightly below the Spanish national average of approximately 80.5 and
86.0 years, respectively (Instituto Nacional de Estadística 2024), and
notably lower than the estimates for the most affluent census tracts
nationally reported by Redondo-Sánchez et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al.
2022) in their census-tract-level analysis. The sex gap of 4.9 years is
comparable to the national figure and reflects the well-documented male
excess mortality from cardiovascular diseases, lung cancer, and external
causes.

The provincial range of life expectancy (0.8 years for men, 0.9 years
for women) is considerably narrower than the socioeconomic gradient
documented by Redondo-Sánchez et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022) at
the census tract level, who found a gap of 3.8 years for men and 3.2
years for women between the most and least deprived census tracts across
Spain. This discrepancy is an expected consequence of the
\textbf{modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP)}: provincial-level
aggregation masks the substantial intra-provincial heterogeneity in both
income and mortality, particularly the urban--rural gradient within
provinces such as Granada or Málaga. The correlation between income and
life expectancy at the provincial level is weak and not statistically
significant (r = 0.21, R\^{}2 = 0.04, p \textgreater{} 0.05), and our
provincial-level estimate of the income--longevity gradient is severely
attenuated compared to the deprivation-specific figures reported by
Redondo-Sánchez et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al. 2022), who used the
Spanish Deprivation Index (SDI) at the census tract level and found a
gap of 3.8 years for men and 3.2 years for women between the most and
least deprived areas, with statistically significant trends across
deprivation quintiles. The contrast between these two findings
highlights the critical importance of geographical scale when studying
socioeconomic inequalities in health.

It is important to note that the methodological approaches differ
between the two studies. Redondo-Sánchez et al. (Redondo-Sánchez et al.
2022) constructed life tables at the census tract level by direct
estimation of mortality rates within deprivation quintiles using
national death registry data linked to population estimates, whereas our
study estimated life expectancy from individual-level longitudinal
follow-up of the 2011 census cohort in a single region (Andalusia),
using the BDLPA. Despite these methodological differences, both studies
converge on the same fundamental finding: a clear and substantial
socioeconomic gradient in life expectancy exists in Spain, and this
gradient is likely larger than what can be detected at coarser
geographical scales.

The cause-of-death analysis confirms the primacy of tumours and
circulatory diseases as the main drivers of premature mortality. The
estimated gains of approximately 4 years (men) and 3 years (women) from
eliminating tumours are similar to those reported for other European
populations (Beltrán-Sánchez et al. 2008). The larger impact of tumours
in men reflects the historically higher smoking prevalence among Spanish
men, though this gap is narrowing in younger cohorts. These findings
align with the educational inequality patterns documented by Regidor et
al. ({Regidor et al.} 2014), where smoking-related cancers and
cardiovascular diseases were identified as key contributors to the
mortality gap.

The Southern European pattern of smaller health inequalities
({Mackenbach et al.} 2018; Regidor et al. 2012) does not imply the
absence of a socioeconomic gradient. Rather, it suggests that in
contexts such as Andalusia, the gradient may operate through different
mechanisms---potentially mediated by the Mediterranean diet, social
support networks, and the universal healthcare system---compared to
Northern or Eastern European countries. Although the income--life
expectancy correlation was weak and non-significant at the provincial
level, the evidence from census-tract-level studies (Redondo-Sánchez et
al. 2022) clearly demonstrates the existence of a socioeconomic
gradient. This reinforces the need for policies that address the social
determinants of health, including income support, employment, education,
and housing, alongside direct healthcare interventions.

\subsection{Methodological Considerations and
Limitations}\label{methodological-considerations-and-limitations}

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, \textbf{life
expectancy estimates are only available at the provincial level}, not at
the census section level. This is the single most important limitation
of the current analysis. The BDLPA individual identifier does not encode
census section codes, and the only geographical variable available in
the person-level file is the province. While the housing file (smv11cau)
contains more detailed geographical codes, these use a proprietary
format that does not match the standard INE CUSEC codes, and the file
covers only 22\% of the sample. Future work should explore record
linkage between the BDLPA and the Padrón Continuo (municipal register)
to recover census section codes for the full sample.

Second, \textbf{life expectancy is calculated from a period life table}
combining mortality experience across different birth cohorts observed
during 2011--2023. This is the standard approach used by the INE and
Eurostat, but it assumes that the age-specific mortality rates observed
during the follow-up period remain constant, which may not hold if
mortality trends are changing rapidly.

Third, the \textbf{sample size limitations} become important when
disaggregating by province and sex. With approximately 40,000--80,000
individuals per province--sex group, the estimates for extreme age bands
(0--4 years and 90+ years) may be unstable due to small numbers of
observed deaths.

Fourth, the \textbf{income data are from 2015--2022} while the mortality
follow-up covers 2011--2023. There is a partial temporal mismatch:
income conditions at the time of the census (2011) may not perfectly
reflect the socioeconomic conditions experienced by individuals
throughout the follow-up period.

Fifth, the \textbf{cause-of-death decomposition assumes independence
between causes}, which is unrealistic. If one cause is eliminated,
individuals who would have died from that cause remain at risk of dying
from other causes; the gain in life expectancy from eliminating multiple
causes is therefore not simply the sum of the individual gains.

\subsection{Public Health
Implications}\label{public-health-implications}

Despite these limitations, RENTASALUD offers a practical tool for
identifying areas with greater social vulnerability and targeting public
health interventions. The application's interactive nature makes it
accessible to policymakers, public health officials, and community
organisations without specialised training in spatial analysis or
demography.

The finding that tumours and circulatory diseases account for
approximately 7 years of potential life expectancy gain in men
highlights the continued importance of cancer screening programmes,
cardiovascular risk prevention, and smoking cessation interventions,
particularly in more deprived areas where these risk factors are
concentrated.

The positive income--life expectancy gradient, even at the coarse
provincial level, reinforces the need for policies that address the
social determinants of health, including income support, employment,
education, and housing, alongside direct healthcare interventions.

\subsection{Future Directions}\label{future-directions}

We plan to extend the RENTASALUD project in several directions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Small-area estimation of life expectancy}: use Bayesian
  hierarchical models (e.g., Fay-Herriot or spatial smoothing models) to
  produce census-section-level life expectancy estimates by borrowing
  strength across neighbouring areas.
\item
  \textbf{Cause-specific mortality mapping}: integrate the mortality
  data by specific cancer sites (colorectal, lung, breast) at the census
  section level using the BYM2 spatial model, as initially outlined in
  the analysis pipeline.
\item
  \textbf{Temporal trends in life expectancy}: update the BDLPA
  follow-up data as additional waves become available and model changes
  in life expectancy over time.
\item
  \textbf{Inequality indices}: incorporate standard inequality measures
  (Gini coefficient, slope index of inequality, relative index of
  inequality) to quantify health disparities across the income
  distribution.
\item
  \textbf{Reproducibility}: the full analysis pipeline is available in
  the GitHub repository, including the
  \texttt{pipeline\_esperanza\_vida\_por\_causa.R} script for life
  expectancy estimation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

RENTASALUD provides an open, interactive tool for exploring social
inequalities in health in Andalusia. Life expectancy at birth is
estimated at 79.5 years for men and 84.4 years for women, with tumours
and circulatory diseases as the leading causes of potential life
expectancy gain. A positive association between income and life
expectancy is observed at the provincial level. The application is
available online and the source code is openly accessible, with the goal
of promoting evidence-based public health decision-making and
facilitating further research on health inequalities in southern Spain.
